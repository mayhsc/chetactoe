01

NOTHING STARTS ON THE BOARD

The board begins empty. Each player holds four pieces — a pawn, a knight, a bishop and a rook — in a reserve beside the board. Getting a piece into play costs you a turn, so your opening moves are decisions about which piece you commit and where.

Nothing is ever removed from the game. A piece is always either on the board or in a reserve, which is why the reserve is worth watching: it tells you exactly what your opponent still has to play with.

02

ONE THING PER TURN

On your turn you do exactly one of two things:

Place a piece from your reserve onto the board, or move a piece you already have in play. You never do both, and you may not pass.

Placing is the only way to get a piece out, and moving is the only way to finish a line. That tension is most of the game: every piece you place is a piece your opponent can see coming, and every piece you hold back is one they have to keep accounting for.

03

HOW EACH PIECE MOVES

As in chess, with one wrinkle: a pawn faces away from you when you place it, moves one square forward, and takes one square diagonally forward — and when it reaches the far edge it turns around and walks back. A pawn is never dead wood. Each board below shows where that piece could go from the middle of an empty board.

A sliding piece stops at the first piece in its way. It can take an enemy standing there; it cannot pass through anything, and it cannot land on one of its own.

04

GET TWO OUT BEFORE YOU MOVE ANYTHING

Until you have two pieces on the board, placing is all you may do. It stops the game opening with one piece shuffling about, and it is the rule that decides who the board favours: with no requirement at all the second player wins more often, and with three the first player runs away with it. Two is the setting that sits level.

That number came out of playing the game a few thousand times rather than out of taste — see the root README.

05

WHERE YOU MAY PLACE

Your first piece goes anywhere. After that, a piece from your reserve must land on a square touching one you already hold — including diagonally. The reserve builds a position; it does not parachute into one.

06

TAKING A PIECE

Move onto an enemy piece and you capture it. It is not removed from the game — it goes back to its owner's reserve, and they can place it again later.

There is a cost, though: a captured piece sits out one turn before it can be placed. So taking something does not win you material, it wins you a tempo — and on a board where a line is three squares long, a tempo is often the whole game.

07

THREE IN A LINE — WALKED IN, NOT DROPPED IN

Three of your pieces in a row, a column or a diagonal wins. Which three does not matter; a pawn counts the same as a rook.

But a placement may never be the move that completes a line. With two pieces lined up and a gap between them, you cannot simply drop the third one in — you have to move a piece that is already on the board into the gap. That takes an extra turn, and it gives your opponent a turn to see it and stop it.

This one rule is why the game works. Without it, making a line is a race, and whoever moves first wins it — measurably: in self-play the first player took more than nine games in ten.

08

THE SECOND PLAYER MAY TAKE THE FIRST MOVE

Moving first is an advantage. Rather than guess at how to compensate for it, the game hands the decision to the player who is behind: after the first move, and only then, the second player may take that position as their own instead of replying to it. The pieces change hands; the game carries on.

It settles itself. If the opening move is a strong one, it gets taken — so the first player learns to open with something even, and neither seat is worth more than the other.

On the board this is the TAKE THIS POSITION button, and it appears for exactly one turn.

09

WHEN NOBODY IS WINNING

Three rules stop a game going nowhere for ever:

The same position three times is a draw. Hitting the move limit is a draw. And a player who has no legal move at all — everything in play boxed in, nothing placeable — loses.

You will rarely see any of them. They exist because an earlier version of this game had none, and more than half of all games between decent players simply never ended.

THAT IS THE WHOLE GAME

Nine rules, four pieces, sixteen squares. The rest is yours.
